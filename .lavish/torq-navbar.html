Three bottom-nav redesigns

Same brief for all three: modern, comfortable, fast. They differ in how much of the current information architecture they are willing to move to get there — and that, not the pixels, is the decision.

36 dpWidth of an idle tab today. Android's minimum touch target is 48 dp.
7Things competing for the bar: 6 tabs plus the profile button.
1 of 7Labels visible at a time — only the active tab spells itself out.
260 msEvery tab you touch reflows all the others. Targets move under your thumb.

Measured, not guessed: the dock is inset 14 px each side and padded 6, so 6 tabs and the profile button share 274 dp. With the active tab taking 2.6 × the flex of the rest, an idle tab lands at roughly 36 dp — narrower than a fingertip, unlabelled, and it shifts sideways as soon as anything is tapped. Every option below starts by giving that space back.

What goes wrong

  • Targets are too small. 36 dp idle tabs miss the 48 dp floor, so mis-taps are a matter of time.
  • Targets move. The capsule steals width from its neighbours, so the icon you aimed at is not where you aimed.
  • Icons carry everything. Medal, dumbbell and flexed-bicep all mean "training" to a new user; only one label shows at a time.
  • Profile is an odd one out. A fixed 46 dp button inside a row of morphing tabs, with no active state of its own.
  • The dock says nothing during a workout — the one moment the app is propped against a bench.

Option A

RecommendedSmall build

Five, spelled out

Stop animating and start labelling. Five equal fifths at 70 dp each, every one with its name under the icon, the active one marked by a lime rail and lime type. Profile becomes the fifth slot as your new avatar — the picture we just shipped, doing work.

What you gain

  • Nothing moves, ever. Muscle memory works from the second session on; a tab is always where it was.
  • Every destination reads itself. No icon literacy required, which is where new users lose a tracker.
  • 70 dp targets — comfortably past the 48 dp floor, thumb-friendly one-handed.
  • Your face on the bar. The avatar is a better profile affordance than a generic glyph, and it confirms who is signed in.

What it costs

  • History and Exercises leave the dock. History gets a "See all" on Home's recents and a link from Stats; Exercises moves under Workout, where the browser already lives.
  • Two extra entry points to build and keep honest.
  • The dock gets a little plainer — the morph was the flashiest thing in the app.

The centre button is the app's only real verb.

Option B

BoldestMedium build

Action dock

Four places and one verb. Working out is not a destination you browse to — it is the thing the app is for — so it stops being a tab and becomes a 56 dp lime button in the middle of the bar. While a session runs it turns into a live timer that drops you back into the set logger from anywhere in the app.

What you gain

  • The main action is the biggest, closest target — dead centre, where the thumb rests.
  • A live session is never lost. The timer is on screen in every tab, one tap from the logger.
  • Only four things to scan. The fastest bar to read of the three.
  • Reads instantly as a 2026 app rather than a 2019 tab bar.

What it costs

  • The biggest IA move. The Workout tab's start screen (quick start, routines, recommended) becomes a sheet the button opens.
  • Three destinations demoted, not two — Stats joins History and Exercises.
  • A centre button that overhangs the dock needs care against the Android nav bar.
  • Most rework of screens you have already finished.

Option C

Keeps everythingLarge build

Adaptive dock

A dock that changes with what you are doing. Idle it is a compact icon rail. During a session the whole bar becomes the session — live dot, elapsed clock, current exercise, Finish. And a long press on any tab peeks its sub-pages, so Ranks keeps You / Friends / Arena and nothing has to be demoted to reach them.

What you gain

  • No destination lost. The only option that changes zero information architecture.
  • The dock earns its 56 dp during a workout, which is when the phone is actually propped on a bench.
  • Deep pages in one gesture — Arena or Friends without walking through Ranks first.
  • Shortest bar of the three, so more page for the content.

What it costs

  • Long press is invisible. It needs a first-run hint, and hidden gestures are always a discoverability bet.
  • Icon-only again — the label problem stays unsolved for new users.
  • A bar that changes shape can feel unstable if the transition is anything less than perfect.
  • The most code and the most states to test.

My read: A fixes the thing that is actually broken — cramped, moving, unlabelled targets — for the least money and with nothing to regret. B is the better product if you believe torq is a workout-first app rather than a dashboard-first one, and it is the one that will look modern longest. They also compose: ship A now, and add B's centre action later without touching A's labels. C is the most interesting and the one I would not build third-party-blind — its long press wants a real device in a real gym before you commit.
